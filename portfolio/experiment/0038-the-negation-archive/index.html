Director's Note

The Negation Archive was established in 2004 following the dissolution of the Institute for Vanishing Studies (Ithaca, NY, est. 1988) and the transfer of its holdings—then numbering some 1,200 catalogue entries—to the Department of Ephemeral Holdings. The collection has since grown to include over 4,600 accessioned items, of which 20 are represented in this exhibition.

The Archive exists to document and preserve records of phenomena that no longer exist in any knowable form. This mandate distinguishes it from related collections concerned with extinct biological species, demolished architecture, or obsolete technology—categories in which physical evidence or living memory persists. The Negation Archive collects only that which has left no trace except its own absence.

The condition of total erasure is itself a form of documentation. What cannot be found has, in a sense, been precisely located.

— I. Colbertsen, Director

Accessions 2004.001 – 2018.047
NA 2004.003
The Experience of Not Knowing What Time It Is
Classification Sensory-cognitive phenomenon Date of Last Known Occurrence c. 2005, rural areas (estimated) Provenance Bequest of the Institute for Vanishing Studies, 2004 Medium Lived experience, absence of timekeeping apparatus
Condition Report

Absent.

Ubiquitous time displays have rendered the condition effectively inaccessible. The Archive has logged 113 oral history testimonies from subjects born before 1955. None could reliably induce it.

Several respondents described a specific quality of the experience—"a kind of unmooring"—that has proven difficult to verify against secondhand accounts. The Archive continues to solicit testimony, though no new credible reports have been received since 2017. Entry remains open pending further documentation.

NA 2004.007
The Sound of a Dial-Up Modem Heard from Another Room
Classification Auditory experience (mediated) Date of Last Known Occurrence c. 2008, North America and Europe Provenance Field collection, Institute for Vanishing Studies Medium Sound, drywall, ambient domestic space
Condition Report

Absent.

While the sound itself has been preserved in digital recordings, the specific phenomenon—hearing it muffled through walls while doing something else, not yet knowing whether the connection would succeed—is no longer reproducible. The experience required uncertainty about connectivity outcomes and a surrounding domestic environment with poor acoustic insulation.

The modulating handshake tones carried affective meaning for a generation: anticipation, patience, dread, relief. The recording alone is a specimen, not an experience. We distinguish between the preserved artifact and the lost phenomenon as rigorously as any natural history collection distinguishes between a type specimen and a living organism.

NA 2004.011 INCOMPLETE RECORD
A Specific Smell, Described by Donor as "the Way August Used to Smell"
Classification Olfactory (unverified) Date of Last Known Occurrence Unknown Provenance Oral testimony, single donor (Anonymous, 2003) Medium [Not recorded]
Condition Report

Unconfirmed. No second source.

The donor was 87 years old at time of testimony. She could not identify the smell's constituents or context, only that it was "the way August used to smell" and that it no longer occurred. No chemical analysis was performed. The entry has been flagged for deaccession review three times (2009, 2014, 2018) but retained on each occasion by committee vote. See: Proceedings of the Ephemeral Holdings Board, 12th and 19th Sessions.

Retained on the argument that an unverified absence is not the same as a confirmed presence. The Board has acknowledged that this reasoning is somewhat circular.

NA 2006.019
The Sensation of Being the Only Person Awake in a House
Classification Sensory-cognitive phenomenon Date of Last Known Occurrence Ongoing (see notes) Provenance Field collection, anonymous donor (2006); expanded with oral testimonies (2008–2015) Medium Lived experience, nocturnal domestic space
Condition Report

Access restricted.

This entry was reclassified in 2016 following a period of internal debate. The phenomenon is not fully absent: it can still be experienced in certain households under certain conditions. However, the proliferation of ambient light (LED indicators, standby lights, charging devices), always-on network connectivity, and the awareness of same has altered the phenomenological ground. The experience as historically documented—characterized by a feeling of total solitude and custodial responsibility for the sleeping household—may no longer be faithfully instantiable.

The restriction is not protective. The phenomenon is not dangerous. The committee simply could not agree on whether it still existed. The minority position (see Dr. A. Veld's dissent, filed separately) held that restriction was "an act of premature mourning dressed as scholarship." The majority held that the catalog's integrity required honest notation of ambiguity. The entry remains the Archive's most-cited and most-contested holding.

NA 2007.009
The Feeling of Being Bored in a Way That Felt Productive
Classification Affective state Date of Last Known Occurrence c. 1995 (pre-broadband internet) Provenance General collection
Condition Report

Absent.

Boredom persists. What has been lost is a specific quality of it—sustained, unstructured, not unpleasant—that sometimes gave way to invention, wandering, or reverie. Current instantiations are shorter in duration and more likely to terminate in compulsive behavior.
NA 2007.014
Awaiting a Letter
Classification Temporal-affective complex Date of Last Known Occurrence Variable; functionally obsolete by 2005 Provenance Transfer from the Postal History Collection, 2007 Medium Anticipation, postal infrastructure
Condition Report

Absent.

Surpassed by instant messages, email, and push notifications. The experience of checking a mailbox with genuine uncertainty—holding the possibility that something desired might or might not arrive today, or this week—required both spatial practice (walking to the box) and temporal dilation (one-day to multi-week delivery windows). The affective texture cannot be replicated by push notifications, which compress anticipation to near-zero duration.

Related entries: NA 2008.033 (The Sound of the Mail Truck), NA 2010.041 (Recognizing Handwriting on an Envelope), NA 2012.008 (Receiving Mail That Was Not a Bill or Advertisement). See cross-reference index, pp. 184–191.

NA 2008.031 ON LOAN
The Silence of a House Where No Device Is On
Classification Auditory environment (domestic) Date of Last Known Occurrence [See curatorial note] Provenance Gift of M. Harwell, 2008 Medium Absence of electrical hum, fan noise, hard drive spin, transformer buzz
Condition Report

Removed from active display. Loaned to the National Sound Archive (UK) for the exhibition "Silence: A Retrospective" (2023–2024).

Harwell's original notes describe the phenomenon as "not silence exactly, because silence isn't the right word. It was the sound the house made when nothing in it was running. The refrigerator was the loudest thing and it was in the other room. You could hear the structure settling. Your own breathing sounded enormous. It wasn't peaceful. It was just what quiet was."

NA 2010.022
The Fear That Your Local Mall Might Be Infinite
Classification Affective state (spatial-cognitive) Date of Last Known Occurrence c. 2005 (w/ regional variation) Provenance General collection Medium Architectural disorientation, consumer environment
Condition Report

Absent.

I am placing this entry under protest. — K. Sorensen, Assistant Cataloguer

[Curatorial note filed by K. Sorensen, 2010:] This phenomenon was proposed by a summer intern who described "a feeling I had as a kid in malls, like the place went on forever and you could get lost and never come back." The description was secondhand memory filtered through childhood imagination. By our own accession criteria, childhood feelings about spatial navigation in commercial environments do not constitute a "phenomenon bearing no known remaining instantiation." I recommended rejection. The committee overruled me, noting that "the specific architectural conditions that produced this affect—the labyrinthine multi-wing mall without smartphone navigation—no longer exist." I maintain that this is a category error and that the intern should have been sent back to their graduate program with clearer instructions. The entry has been included in the exhibition. I have been asked to note that the committee's decision was unanimous.

NA 2011.004
Being Lost
Classification Spatio-cognitive state Date of Last Known Occurrence c. 2012 (urban); 2015 (rural) Provenance Major field collection; 400+ oral testimonies Medium Absence of wayfinding information, embodied navigation
Condition Report

Absent.

The Archive considers this one of its most significant holdings. "Being lost" as a sustained state—lasting minutes, hours, occasionally days—was a common human experience for millennia. GPS-enabled mobile mapping has reduced the duration of the lost state to near-zero in most inhabited areas. The phenomenological texture of being lost (anxiety, heightened perception, spatial reasoning, the slow construction of mental maps, the experience of recovery) has no remaining vehicle of access.

Several respondents independently used the same phrase: "the world was bigger when you could get lost in it." The entry has produced more published scholarship than any other in the collection. See: Harwell & Veld, The Cartography of Absence (2014); Colbertsen, "What We Lost When We Stopped Getting Lost," Speculative Anthropology Review (2016).

NA 2012.015
Making Eye Contact with Strangers Without Either Party Feeling Obligated to Acknowledge It Digitally
Classification Social phenomenon Date of Last Known Occurrence Ongoing, but altered beyond recognition Provenance Anonymous donor, 2012 Medium Public space, mutual gaze, shared anonymity
Condition Report

Transferred to Long-Term Observation, 2017.

The committee elected not to classify this entry as "absent" but rather to place it under long-term observation, noting that the phenomenon persists in attenuated form but has been "so thoroughly infiltrated by the awareness of potential digital replication (photography, social media posting, facial recognition) that the original condition of unmediated mutual anonymity may no longer be achievable in urban environments." Rural field studies are ongoing.

The donor's original description noted "a specific kind of meeting eyes with someone on a bus or in a waiting room where you both just... looked. And then looked away. And nothing happened. Nothing was supposed to happen. It was just two people existing near each other." The Archive has recorded 47 versions of this description from independent sources. The consistency is notable.

NA 2014.016
The Experience of Telling Someone a Piece of News
Classification Interpersonal phenomenon Date of Last Known Occurrence c. 2014 (w/ socioeconomic variation) Provenance General collection
Condition Report

Absent.

The act of being the first to inform someone of an event—delivering news that the recipient did not already know—has been largely superseded by the simultaneous reception of information through shared digital feeds. The specific social dynamic (sender authority, recipient surprise, the temporal gap between event and awareness) no longer obtains in most contexts.
NA 2015.007 STATUS UNDER REVIEW
The Smell of Photocopies
Classification Olfactory experience (institutional/occupational) Date of Last Known Occurrence Unknown; possibly ongoing in limited settings Provenance Donated by R. Thomason, office supply company (ret.), 2015
Condition Report

Status: UNCONFIRMED.

The donor insists the phenomenon has vanished. Our cataloguing staff have located three operating photocopiers in the metropolitan area that still produce the characteristic odor (ozone, toner particulate, heated paper). Thomason has been invited to verify. He has not responded to correspondence since 2016. The entry remains in limbo. A field visit has been scheduled and cancelled four times.

[Addendum, 2019:] Thomason's postal forwarding expired. His last known address returned mail marked "no longer at this location." We do not know if he still smells photocopiers. We do not know if he still exists in a locatable sense. The entry stays.

Deaccessioned: The Satisfying Thwack of a Cassette Tape Clicking into a Car Stereo
Classification Tactile-auditory experience Date of Last Known Occurrence c. 2005 Provenance General collection Status Deaccessioned 2017
Condition Report

Deaccessioned following review. The physical action described was determined to be a "mechanical interaction with a tangible artifact" rather than a "phenomenon bearing no remaining instantiation." NA 2016.041
The Unease of Not Knowing Whether a Photograph Had Come Out
Classification Affective-cognitive state Date of Last Known Occurrence c. 2004 (consumer film photography) Provenance Anonymous donor, 2016 Medium Film processing latency, uncertainty, hope
Condition Report

Absent.

Digital photography provides immediate visual confirmation. The experience of taking a photograph and then waiting—hours, days, or weeks—to discover whether it was exposed correctly, composed as intended, or ruined by motion blur, closed eyes, or overexposure, has no remaining instantiation in standard consumer practice. Professional film photographers report that even their experience is altered by the knowledge that digital alternatives exist.
NA 2017.005
Calling a Friend Without Knowing Whether They Were Home
Classification Social-affective practice Date of Last Known Occurrence c. 2010 Provenance Oral testimony collection, 2017. 83 respondents. Medium Landline telephone, uncertainty, shared schedules
Condition Report

Absent.

Before mobile phones and persistent digital presence, calling someone's home phone was an act of faith. They might answer. They might not. They might not be home. The phone would ring and ring. You had no way to know. What was lost was not the phone call but the not-knowing.

Seventeen respondents independently described "the sound of the phone ringing in an empty house" as something they could almost physically remember. "The other person's phone, ringing where they weren't." Three respondents became emotional during testimony. The Archive does not comment on the emotional responses of its donors, but it notes them.

NA 2018.047
The Belief That Some Things Would Always Be There
Classification Cognitive-affective orientation Date of Last Known Occurrence Uncertain. Ongoing investigation. Provenance Donated by the Department of Ephemeral Holdings, en bloc, 2018 Medium Expectation, constancy, implicit trust in continuity
Condition Report

The Archive is not certain this entry belongs here.

It may be the case that the belief described—the unnamed confidence that certain features of the world were permanent and would persist without the need for attention or protection—was never a discrete phenomenon but rather a background condition of consciousness. If so, it cannot be cataloged any more than one can catalog the medium in which the catalog itself is printed. The committee voted 5–3 to accession. The dissenting position held that cataloging this entry was "an act of self-description by an institution that has begun to mistake itself for its contents." The majority responded that this was precisely the point.

This is the most recent entry in the exhibition. It is displayed last not by accession number but by editorial decision. The curator has declined to explain the ordering.
