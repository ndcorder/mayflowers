# Contained Intimacy: Affective Performance and Temporal Compression in Commercial Transit Spaces

**Dr. Lena Voss**
Department of Cognitive Science, Emory University
Group for Interaction Analytics and Social Topology (GIAST)

Pre-print draft. Not for citation without author permission.

## Abstract

This paper presents findings from a six-month embedded ethnographic study (March–August 2024) examining how strangers negotiate intimacy within the temporal and spatial constraints of commercial ride-share environments. The researcher completed 847 rides as a certified driver across two platforms in the greater Atlanta metropolitan area, collecting observational, behavioral, and self-reported affective data from 1,204 passengers. Results indicate that the seventeen-minute average ride duration constitutes a uniquely structured social container in which the brevity of anticipated contact, combined with the confessional architecture of the vehicle interior, produces disclosures of personal information that exceed baseline norms for stranger interaction by a factor of 3.7. We propose a model of "temporary confessional intimacy" to describe this phenomenon, distinct from both parasocial and conventional social bonding. Limitations of the study design and implications for theories of bounded social interaction are discussed.

*Keywords: intimacy, stranger interaction, spatial semiotics, automotive space, confessional behavior, ethnography, ride-share*

## I. Introduction

When Uber Technologies introduced its ride-sharing platform in 2009, it inadvertently created one of the most widely deployed confessional spaces in human history. This claim is not made lightly. Prior to the advent of commercial ride-sharing, sustained stranger-to-stranger interaction in enclosed spaces occurred primarily in therapeutic settings (the analyst's office), punitive settings (the interrogation room), and incidental settings (the elevator, the waiting room). Each carries well-documented social scripts. The ride-share vehicle, however, occupies a liminal category: neither fully public nor fully private, both commercial service and interpersonal encounter, with a mean duration of seventeen minutes and four seconds—long enough to disclose something meaningful, short enough to render that disclosure consequence-free.

The question motivating this research was straightforward: What happens in the seventeen minutes?

The extant literature on commercial intimacy (Hochschild, 1983; Murphy, 1998) addresses service workers who perform emotional labor as a job requirement. Flight attendants, hotel staff, therapists—their emotional work is continuous, occupational, structured by institutional norms. The ride-share driver occupies a different category. Passengers enter the vehicle aware that they will exit it permanently in a matter of minutes. This finitude appears to alter the social contract in ways that have not been adequately theorized.

Section II outlines the study's methodology. Sections III through V present subject profiles, behavioral observations, and statistical findings. Section VI discusses implications and limitations. A brief post-study addendum follows.

## II. Methodology

### II.1. Research Design

The researcher completed the standard onboarding process for both Uber and Lyft platforms in the state of Georgia, including background check, vehicle inspection, and platform-specific orientation modules. No deception was employed regarding the researcher's identity; all passengers consented to audio recording and observational notation through a prominently displayed consent placard on the rear passenger-side window. The placard read:

> "Academic Study in Progress: This vehicle is equipped with recording devices for an Emory University research project on social interaction in transit spaces. By entering this vehicle, you consent to being observed and recorded. Data will be anonymized. For questions, contact [email redacted]."

A total of 89 passengers (7.4% of the sample) declined to participate upon reading the placard. These individuals were transported to their destinations but excluded from all data collection.

### II.2. Data Collection

Three primary data streams were collected:

1. **Audio recordings** of all passenger verbalizations, subsequently transcribed and coded for disclosure content, affective valence, and topical category.
2. **Behavioral observation matrices** completed post-ride by the researcher, documenting physical comportment, seating position, phone interaction, eye contact, and exit behavior.
3. **Self-reported affective state surveys** administered via QR code at ride conclusion (completion rate: 31%).

All coding was performed by the researcher and two graduate assistants blind to the study's hypotheses. Inter-rater reliability was assessed via Cohen's kappa (κ = 0.84, 95% CI [0.81, 0.87]).

### II.3. Parameters and Definitions

For the purposes of this study, "intimacy" is operationalized according to the Prager (1995) relational intimacy framework, which identifies five behavioral indicators: self-disclosure (voluntary sharing of personal information), affective sharing (emotional expression directed toward the interaction partner), physical closeness (spatial behavior exceeding social norms), relational continuity gestures (references to future interaction or ongoing connection), and vulnerability displays (exposure of weakness, uncertainty, or emotional need).

A "confessional event" is coded when a passenger discloses information rated at Level 3 or above on the Revised Self-Disclosure Index (RSDI), indicating content that would typically be reserved for close friends, family members, or therapeutic professionals.

### II.4. Researcher Position

In grounded ethnographic work, the researcher's subjectivity constitutes both instrument and limitation. I note here—because it will become relevant in later sections—that I am thirty-nine years old, female, white, and maintain a physical presentation that multiple passengers independently described as "neutral" or "hard to read." I drive a 2019 Honda Civic. I did not alter my appearance, speech patterns, or conversational behavior for the study beyond what the platform naturally induces (greetings, route confirmations, pleasantries).

I should also note that I had never driven professionally prior to this study. I obtained my driver's license at age seventeen in Columbus, Ohio. I have never received a traffic citation. This will seem like a strange detail to include in a methodology section. I include it because it is the kind of fact that passengers extracted from me, repeatedly, and because it matters that I was surprised by how much I liked answering.

## III. Subject Profiles

What follows are condensed behavioral summaries of six representative passengers, selected from the 1,204-person sample. These profiles are intended to ground the statistical analysis in lived encounter. Names and identifying details have been altered.

### Subject 204

Female, estimated age 50–55. Boarded at Emory University Hospital, dropped at a residential address in Decatur. Duration: 22 minutes. Introduced herself as "Dee" without being asked. Held her phone in her left hand at chest height for the entire ride but never looked at it—the screen was dark, and her thumb moved across it in small, repetitive circles, a gesture I came to think of as a rosary. Asked what music I liked. When I said I didn't keep music on during rides, she said, "Good. Music is a way of not hearing someone." Then she told me about her son. He was twenty-eight. He had been admitted to the hospital three days prior with kidney failure. She used the phrase "kidney failure" and then corrected herself—"no, renal failure, that's what the doctors say, they always have a cleaner word." She did not cry. She sat very still. She said, "They always have a cleaner word for everything that's happening to the people you love." When we arrived at her destination, she asked if she could sit in the car for a moment. I said yes. She sat for approximately ninety seconds, still holding her phone at chest height, still moving her thumb. Then she said, "Thank you for the silence," and exited.

I rated this interaction as a Level 5 confessional event on the RSDI, which requires disclosure of "acute personal crisis accompanied by affective vulnerability." But I note here—in violation of the coding protocol I myself designed—that what struck me was not the disclosure but the thumb. The small circular motion on a dark screen. I have replayed this gesture in my mind more often than is methodologically justifiable.

### Subject 511

Male, estimated age 32–35. Boarded at a bar in Buckhead, dropped at the Westin Peachtree Plaza. Duration: 14 minutes. Did not greet me. Slid into the rear seat on the driver's side, behind me, which only 11% of passengers do. Immediately placed a baseball cap over his face and said, "Don't talk to me." I confirmed the destination and said nothing further. At minute six, he removed the cap and said, "I'm sorry. That was rude." I said it was fine. He said, "No, it's—I just came from seeing my daughter and I'm not supposed to—" Then he stopped. Put the cap back over his face. Did not speak again. Exited at the hotel without saying goodbye.

I coded this as a Level 2 event (fragmentary disclosure, incomplete affective arc). My graduate assistant coded it as Level 3 (partial disclosure of significant personal information). We argued about this. She said the fragment "I'm not supposed to" carried an implicit Level 4 (legal or custodial disclosure). I said we couldn't code implications. She said that was exactly what coding meant. This was the only disagreement we were unable to resolve through discussion. In the final dataset, Subject 511 is coded as 2.5. There is no 2.5 on the RSDI.

### Subject 623

Female, estimated age 28. Boarded at Hartsfield-Jackson airport, dropped at an apartment complex in Sandy Springs. Duration: 31 minutes (heavy traffic on I-85). Placed a small canvas tote bag on the seat beside her, buckled her seatbelt, then placed her left hand flat on top of the tote bag and left it there for the entire ride, as if the bag might escape. Within four minutes of boarding, she asked, "Do you ever feel like you're arriving somewhere that isn't going to have you?" I said, "Sometimes." She said, "I moved here for a man who is currently not answering his phone." She then told me—in a tone remarkably similar to the tone one uses to read a grocery list—that she had quit her job in Minneapolis, broken her lease, and driven her belongings to Goodwill because he had said, "Just bring what you can carry." She had carried a tote bag. The tote bag was now under her hand. She had been trying to call him for three hours. She asked me if I thought "just bring what you can carry" was a metaphor or a logistical instruction. I said I wasn't sure. She said, "Neither am I, but I think the answer matters."

When we arrived at the apartment complex, she did not exit. She called him again. I could hear it ringing through her phone speaker. It rang twelve times. She hung up, looked at me in the rearview mirror, and said, "Would you mind driving me to a hotel?" I drove her to the Holiday Inn Express on Roswell Road. She carried the tote bag into the lobby. I watched through the glass doors as she approached the front desk.

I coded this as a Level 5 confessional event. I also—for reasons I did not document at the time and cannot fully reconstruct—waited in the parking lot for nine minutes to make sure she came back out of the lobby. She did not come back out. I drove home.

### Subject 707

Male, estimated age 70–75. Boarded at a VA medical center, dropped at a residence in Marietta. Duration: 26 minutes. Wore a suit. Not sport coat—suit. Charcoal, well-fitted, with a lapel pin that I initially believed was an American flag but, upon closer inspection in the rearview mirror, was a small bronze replica of a military aircraft. He sat in the rear passenger seat, diagonally across from me, which is the seating position adopted by only 4% of the sample and is associated with the highest rates of sustained eye contact. He asked my name. I told him. He said, "Lena. That's a good name. My wife's name was Lena." I said, "Was?" He said, "Eleven years." Then he looked out the window for a long time. At minute eighteen, he said, "I'm going to a funeral." I said I was sorry. He said, "It's not hers. It's my dentist's." He paused. "I didn't know what to wear to your dentist's funeral. So I wore what I would have worn to hers." He adjusted his lapel pin. He said, "She would have thought that was strange. Going to your dentist's funeral. She would have come with me, though." At the destination, he shook my hand through the open rear window and said, "Thank you for letting me talk about her. People stop letting you talk about them after a while."

I coded this as a Level 5. The graduate assistants coded it as Level 5. We did not argue. I wrote down his description of the lapel pin in my field notes and then, later that night, searched for bronze aircraft lapel pins online. I found several. I did not purchase one. I am noting this for reasons that will become clear.

### Subject 841

Female, estimated age 20–22. Boarded at Georgia Tech campus, dropped at a residential address in Midtown. Duration: 11 minutes. Entered the vehicle already crying—not the quiet kind, but the kind where the person has clearly been crying for some time and has reached a state of respiratory exhaustion. She said, "Hi, sorry, I'm fine, I'm just—" and then could not complete the sentence. I offered tissues. She took one, held it in her fist without using it. She stared at her phone, where a text conversation was visible. I could not read the content and did not attempt to. She typed a message, deleted it, typed another, deleted it, locked the phone, unlocked it, and typed a third message, which she sent. Then she said, "Do you ever send a text you know is the wrong text? Like you know it's going to make everything worse but you send it because at least then something will happen?" I said, "Yes." She looked at me in the mirror and said, "What did you do after?" I said, "I waited." She said, "For what?" I said, "For whatever came next." She nodded. She unlocked her phone, looked at it, and said, "Nothing came next." Then she exited at her destination.

I coded this as a Level 4 (disclosure of emotional distress with request for guidance or counsel). But I should note—again, in violation of my own protocol—that this is the only passenger in the study whose question I answered with a personal response rather than a neutral prompt. She asked, "What did you do after?" I said, "I waited." This was not a neutral prompt. This was an answer. I do not know why I gave it.

### Subject 889

Male, estimated age 40–45. Boarded at a MARTA station, dropped at an office park in Alpharetta. Duration: 19 minutes. Did not speak for the first eleven minutes. During this time, he made and received three phone calls, all in a language I could not identify but which my audio transcription service flagged as Amharic. During the first call, his affect was neutral. During the second, it was animated. During the third, he became visibly upset—his voice rose, he gripped the door handle with his free hand, and at one point he said, in English, "No, no, no, listen to me, listen—" before switching back to Amharic. After hanging up, he sat in silence for thirty seconds, then said to me, without any preceding context: "My brother is going to lose his restaurant." I said I was sorry to hear that. He said, "He borrowed money from people you should not borrow money from, and now they want the restaurant, and I have the money to save it, but if I give him the money, my wife will leave me, because I promised her—because we agreed—" He stopped. He put his hands flat on his knees. He looked at me in the rearview mirror and said, "You don't know me. I don't know you. In nineteen minutes, you will drop me off and we will never see each other again." I said, "Yes." He said, "So I can tell you the truth. I am going to give him the money. I know my wife will leave. I know she should leave. I'm going to do it anyway, because he is my brother, and that is the only reason I have." He paused, then said, "But you can't tell anyone, because you don't know me." I said I wouldn't. He said, "I know you won't. That's why I told you."

I coded this as a Level 5. I did not tell anyone. I am now publishing it in an academic journal. I have changed his name, his destination, his language, his ethnicity, his brother's business, and his stated reason for giving the money. But I have told his secret, which I promised not to do. I am not comfortable with this. I am doing it anyway.

## IV. Behavioral Observations

Table 1 presents aggregate behavioral data across the six-month study period.

**Table 1: Passenger Behavior Frequencies (N = 1,204)**

| Behavior | Frequency | % |
|---|---|---|
| Spontaneous greeting | 887 | 73.7% |
| Spontaneous disclosure (Level 3+) | 412 | 34.2% |
| Phone interaction (sustained, >50% of ride) | 563 | 46.8% |
| Rear-driver seat selection | 132 | 11.0% |
| Rear-passenger seat selection | 941 | 78.2% |
| Front passenger seat selection | 131 | 10.9% |
| Eye contact via rearview mirror | 691 | 57.4% |
| Physical object from bag/pocket (unrelated to trip) | 298 | 24.8% |
| Request to change music | 187 | 15.5% |
| Request for silence | 94 | 7.8% |
| Disclosure of acute crisis (Level 5) | 87 | 7.2% |
| Farewell exceeding one word | 746 | 62.0% |
| Delayed exit (>10 seconds after vehicle stop) | 223 | 18.5% |
| Touch (handshake, shoulder, etc.) | 41 | 3.4% |

Several findings merit preliminary discussion. The rate of Level 5 confessional disclosure (7.2%) is, as noted in the abstract, 3.7 times higher than baseline stranger disclosure rates observed in comparable confined-space studies (see Kendon & Ferber, 1973; Greenbaum & Rosenfeld, 1980). The most common topic for high-level disclosure was, unexpectedly, not romantic relationships (22% of Level 5 events) but parental or familial duty conflicts (31%), followed by workplace identity crises (19%), health anxieties (14%), and miscellaneous (14%).

The delayed exit behavior (18.5%) was not anticipated by the study design and emerged during preliminary data review. Passengers who delayed exit were 4.6 times more likely to have produced a Level 4 or Level 5 confessional event during the ride. This correlation is significant at p < .001 and suggests that the vehicle functions not only as a confessional space but as a transitional holding environment—a place where passengers can complete an emotional arc before re-entering the social world.

It was during the coding of delayed exit behavior that I first noticed the anomaly in my own data. I have chosen to include it here rather than suppress it, as suppression would constitute a more serious methodological violation than the anomaly itself.

**Table 2: Researcher Behavior Anomalies (unplanned)**

| Behavior | Mar | Apr | May | Jun | Jul | Aug |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total rides | 112 | 138 | 142 | 151 | 147 | 157 |
| Rides extending beyond optimal route | 0 | 2 | 5 | 8 | 13 | 19 |
| Post-dropoff waiting (mean seconds) | 0 | 0 | 4.2 | 9.1 | 17.3 | 24.8 |
| Personal disclosures exceeding protocol | 0 | 1 | 3 | 7 | 9 | 14 |
| Post-shift circuitous return routing | 0 | 0 | 0 | 2 | 6 | 11 |

I want to be clear about what Table 2 describes. "Rides extending beyond optimal route" means I chose longer routes when passengers were disclosing at Level 4 or above. "Post-dropoff waiting" means I remained parked outside a passenger's destination and watched them enter their building. "Personal disclosures exceeding protocol" means I answered a passenger's question with a personal response rather than a neutral prompt, as in the case of Subject 841. "Post-shift circuitous return routing" means that, after ending my scheduled driving shift for the evening, I did not drive directly home but instead drove through the city with no passenger, no destination, and no data collection purpose, sometimes for up to forty-five minutes.

These behaviors were not part of the study. They were not authorized. They emerged, and I did not stop them, and I did not record them until a post-hoc review of GPS logs and dashcam footage in September 2024 forced me to acknowledge them.

## V. Statistical Analysis

A hierarchical linear model was fitted to predict confessional depth (RSDI score) as a function of ride duration, time of day, passenger sex, passenger estimated age, seating position, and researcher verbal prompt frequency. The full model accounted for 38.2% of the variance in RSDI scores (R² = .382, F(7, 1196) = 105.3, p < .001).

Key predictors:

- **Ride duration**: β = .31, p < .001. Each additional minute of ride time increased the probability of Level 3+ disclosure by 6.2%.
- **Late-night rides** (10 PM–2 AM): β = .24, p < .001. Late-night rides produced higher RSDI scores independent of duration.
- **Rear-passenger seating** (diagonal position): β = .19, p < .01. Passengers who sat diagonally behind the driver disclosed at higher rates than those who sat behind the passenger seat.
- **Researcher prompt frequency**: β = .07, p = .14 (n.s.). The researcher's verbal prompts did not significantly predict disclosure depth, suggesting that passengers' confessional behavior was driven by the environmental affordances of the space rather than by social facilitation.

This last finding is important. It was not my conversation that opened passengers. It was the container.

## VI. Discussion

The data support the hypothesis that commercial ride-share vehicles function as confessional spaces—a term I use with deliberate reference to Goffman's (1959) concept of the "back region" but with a crucial modification. The traditional confessional (religious, therapeutic, juridical) involves an authority figure who receives the disclosure. The ride-share driver is not an authority figure. The ride-share driver is a temporary and anonymous listener whose defining characteristic is imminent disappearance.

This changes the social function of the disclosure. When Subject 889 told me about his brother's restaurant, he was not seeking advice, absolution, or intervention. He was seeking the experience of utterance itself—the relief of having said a true thing to another human being who would carry it without consequence. He said, "You don't know me." This was not a statement of distance. It was a statement of permission.

The theory of temporary confessional intimacy that emerges from this data holds that the seventeen-minute ride functions as a bounded epistemic container: a space in which knowledge can be transferred without the social obligations that normally accompany intimate knowledge. When you tell your therapist a secret, the therapist is obligated to respond. When you tell your spouse a secret, the spouse is obligated to remember. When you tell a ride-share driver a secret, the driver is obligated only to drive. This asymmetry—full disclosure on one side, minimal obligation on the other—appears to be precisely the condition that enables the disclosure.

But I need to address what is happening in Table 2, because it complicates the theory in ways I did not anticipate and have not fully resolved.

The theory predicts that the driver functions as a neutral receiving surface—a kind of acoustic tile for emotional projection. But my behavioral data suggests that I was not neutral. I was extending routes, waiting outside buildings, answering personal questions, and driving aimlessly through the city after my shifts ended. I was, in other words, behaving like someone who did not want the container to empty. I was behaving like someone who had become invested in the function of the container itself.

I have spent several months trying to determine whether this investment constitutes an experimental confound or a finding. I have come to believe it is both.

The confessional space does something to the confessor. But it also, apparently, does something to the confessional.

## VII. Post-Study Addendum

The study officially concluded on August 31, 2024. On September 1, I intended to return to my normal academic schedule. I drove to campus, parked in my assigned space, and went to my office. At 5:47 PM, I left my office, walked to the parking deck, and opened the Lyft driver app. I drove for three hours. I collected no data. I did not record the rides. I did not code any confessional events. I did not need to. I knew what was happening. I was sitting in the container, and the container was empty, and I was waiting for someone to come and fill it, because I had learned—over six months and 847 rides and 1,204 strangers—that the container was the only place where I existed in a particular way that I had not previously known I needed to exist.

This is not a finding. This is not data. This is something that happened to me while I was studying something that happened to other people, and I do not have a framework for it, and I do not have a theory, and I am not going to develop one, because I think that developing a theory about why I cannot stop driving strangers around Atlanta would be another way of not hearing what is actually being said.

What is actually being said, I think, is this: I am thirty-nine. I am a cognitive scientist with a secure position and a publication record I am proud of. I have been told, by people who are qualified to judge, that I am good at my work. But I drive a 2019 Honda Civic, and seventeen minutes is enough time to hear someone say "thank you for letting me talk about her," and I was not prepared for what that did to me.

As of this writing, I have driven 214 additional rides since the study ended. I have not collected data on any of them. I am not studying the passengers. I do not remember most of their faces. But I remember the thumb moving on the dark screen (Subject 204). I remember the cap pulled over the face (Subject 511). I remember the hand on the tote bag (Subject 623). I remember the aircraft lapel pin (Subject 707). I remember the text sent to no reply (Subject 841). I remember "you don't know me" (Subject 889).

I have not purchased a bronze aircraft lapel pin.

I checked again this morning.
