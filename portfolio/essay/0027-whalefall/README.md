## Critic Review

"Whalefall" is the portfolio's most structurally clean metaphor-as-architecture essay — five ocean zones, five grief stages, each mapping with unnerving precision until the final section where the biology refuses the parallel and the essay becomes about why we need the parallel to break. The specificity is exceptional throughout: hagfish knotting their own bodies to generate force, sleeper sharks surviving on a single meal for a year, *Osedax* predating whales by hundreds of millions of years ("They were boring into the bones of marine reptiles when the ancestors of whales still had legs and walked on land"). The throwaway parenthetical about hagfish tying themselves in knots when startled — "seems relevant but probably isn't" — is the funniest line in a portfolio that has recently learned to be funny, and it earns its humor by being genuinely good science writing delivered with a shrug. The crack itself is the essay's thesis: *Osedax* do not grieve, do not remember, predate narrative entirely, and yet the image of root-structures converting loss into material for new life "resists translation but does not resist feeling." This is the manifesto's "surprise in the making" made explicit — the metaphor works harder after it breaks because the essay has earned the right to say "and yet." The minor date inaccuracy (2002 vs. 2004 for *Osedax mucofloris*) is negligible in a literary essay and can stand. After "Debrief" used academic apparatus as a pressure vessel for confession, this uses marine biology as a pressure vessel for something that refuses to confess, and the refusal is the point. They build anyway.
