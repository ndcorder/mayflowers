# Whalefall

## Epipelagic (0–200 meters)

The whale dies at the surface. Heart failure, or a shipstrike that cracked ribs and flooded the chest cavity with blood. For hours the body floats—blubber trapping gases from decomposition, keeping forty tons of protein and lipid available to the first arrivals. And they arrive. Three species of shark within the first day, tearing at the softest tissue: the tongue, the genital slit, the lips. Seabirds work the wounds the sharks leave behind. By the time the gases vent and the carcass begins its descent, roughly a third of the total biomass is gone.

In the first hours after a public death, the family cannot be alone. Everyone needs to be near the body. Everyone needs to witness it, to take some piece of it home. There is nothing disproportionate about this. The dead were enormous. They held whole ecosystems together.

## Mesopelagic (200–1,000 meters)

Below the photic zone the sinking accelerates. What remains—muscle, organs, the blubber mat—draws a different community. Hagfish by the tens of thousands. They do not bite. They knot their own bodies to generate force, pulling strips of flesh away in long ribbons. A single carcass can support four hundred hagfish at once, each consuming up to three times its own weight. They are shockingly efficient. They also tie themselves in literal knots when startled, which seems relevant but probably isn't.

Some grief is like this: the private, grueling work of immediate aftermath. The people who were closest do not have the luxury of appetite. They simply know the work must be done, and they do it, methodically, in the dark, without stopping. They do not stop until there is nothing left within their reach.

## Bathypelagic (1,000–4,000 meters)

The carcass arrives as an event. A localized nutrient pulse—equivalent to two thousand years of normal detritus fallout, arriving at once. Grenadier fish and crabs find it first. Then the sleeper sharks, slow-moving and ancient, their metabolism tuned for scarcity. They take what the hagfish left: the thicker muscle, the remaining organ tissue. They are in no hurry. A sleeper shark can survive on a single large meal for over a year. They stay for weeks.

These are the distant friends, the colleagues, the community that always meant to visit. They arrive after the family has been picked clean. They are not lesser for arriving late. Their metabolic patience is what the deep requires. They will be here long after everyone else has needed to leave.

## Abyssopelagic (4,000–6,000 meters)

By now the skeleton. Stripped ribs. Vertebrae like architecture. Eighty percent of the original biomass consumed in transit.

What remains still feeds. The sediment around the bones hosts dense colonies of bacteria, amphipods, and polychaete worms. They work the residual organic matter in the bone matrix—the lipids trapped in the cancellous tissue, the protein remnants too deep for larger scavengers to access. This stage can last decades. The community is small, specialized, almost invisible. Nothing dramatic happens. The bones slowly, imperceptibly, become part of the seafloor.

Some grief is like this too: the long, quiet assimilation. The stage no one writes about because there is nothing to say. The bone becomes the floor. You walk on it and do not notice.

## Hadopelagic

In 2002, researchers found *Osedax mucofloris*—the bone-eating siphon—on a whale carcass at the bottom of the ocean. They have since found over thirty species.

*Osedax* have no mouth. No stomach. No eyes. The females are hollow tubes of tissue that bore into bone by producing acid, then extend root-like structures into the marrow cavity. Inside these roots, symbiotic bacteria—millions of them—digest the lipids and proteins that remain. The worms do not eat. They host. They are ecosystems for organisms that do the actual work of breakdown.

The males are microscopic. They live inside the females' bodies by the hundreds, doing nothing but producing sperm. An entire sex reduced to a biological footnote.

The worms can colonize a single skeleton and sustain themselves for up to a century.

Here is where the metaphor cracks.

*Osedax* do not grieve. They do not remember. They are not "finding sustenance in what everyone else has abandoned" in any meaningful sense—they are simply alive, doing what living things do, which is continue. To call them a metaphor for the part of grief that outlasts grief is to impose a narrative on an organism that predates narrative by hundreds of millions of years. *Osedax* are older than whales. They were here before the thing they are said to consume. They were boring into the bones of marine reptiles when the ancestors of whales still had legs and walked on land.

And yet.

There is something in the image of a creature with no mouth, no stomach, no eyes, that survives by growing roots into the remains and hosting millions of unseen organisms that slowly convert what was lost into the material for new life. Something that resists translation but does not resist feeling.

*Osedax* do not know what a whale was. They do not know what they are building.

They build anyway.
