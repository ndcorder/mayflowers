# Annotation of a One-Star Review of Sunridge Hospice and Palliative Care

## The Source Text

The nurses were nice enough I guess but they kept saying things like "comfort measures only" which is basically code for "we're not even trying." The food was terrible and cold everytime and she could barely swallow anyway so whats the point. My brother drove 3 hrs from Reno and nobody even showed him where the vending machine was. We asked multiple times for a second opinion and Dr. Patel just kept saying "her body is shutting down" like thats an answer. My mother deserved better then this place. My family will not be donateing. Would not recommend.

## Annotations

**Would NOT recommend!!!**

The review opens with a sentence fragment that has become Yelp's primary speech act: the verdict before evidence, the conclusion in search of a trial. The capitalization of NOT performs a version of shouting that exists only in writing — vocal volume transliterated into orthographic emphasis. The three exclamation points form a tricolon, a rhetorical device dating to classical antiquity, here deployed to express what the reviewer experiences as incommunicable betrayal.

The phrase "would not recommend" appears in the subjunctive mood, the grammatical register of hypothetical action. The reviewer has been asked by the platform: *Would you recommend this business?* A consumer question. The answer it received was a grief-stricken cry.

This sentence will recur, slightly altered, at the review's end, forming a ring composition — an ancient structuring device found in the *Iliad*, in medieval dream visions, in certain forms of rabbinic commentary. The Yelp review of Sunridge Hospice and Palliative Care is, without its author's knowledge, formally identical to a lament.

---

**My mother was only there for 9 days**

The numeral 9, rather than the word "nine," is the review's first orthographic choice of note. Numerals in prose accelerate reading — the eye skips from word to symbol to word, experiencing the quantity as a visual fact rather than a sounded syllable. Nine. In numerology, a number of completion. In fetal development, the months of gestation. The reviewer's mother was in the hospice for the inverse of a pregnancy, a period of un-creation lasting exactly as long as creation once took.

The word "only" contains the entire grievance. Nine days was insufficient. Insufficient for what, the sentence does not say. For recovery? For adequate preparation? For the dying process to have been lingered in, accommodated, made generous with time? The reviewer measures their mother's stay against an unknown standard — a duration that would have been acceptable, a length of dying that would have felt like enough.

There is no grammatical object in this sentence for the mother to have inhabited. The construction "was only there" withholds the room, the building, the institution, the dying itself. "There" is a spatial void. "Was" places her in a past that has already concluded — a mother who "was" and is no longer even "there."

---

**and they basically gave up on her right away.**

The coordinate conjunction "and" links the mother's nine-day stay to the staff's failure, as though these were events in the same causal sequence: *she was there, and they gave up*. The logic is not temporal but accusatory.

The adverb "basically" functions here as it often does in informal American speech: not as a modifier indicating fundamentality, but as a hedge against the extremity of what follows. To say "they gave up on her" is a serious charge against medical professionals. To say "they *basically* gave up on her" is to make the same charge while requesting that it not be heard as quite so severe — or, paradoxically, to increase its severity by suggesting that the giving-up was so thorough it permeated even the institution's most basic operations.

"Right away" compresses time in a manner that conflicts with the earlier "only 9 days." The giving-up occurred immediately; the nine days passed in a state of already-having-given-up. The phrase belongs to the register of customer service complaints — *I called and they basically told me no right away* — and its appearance in a review of a facility where someone died produces a category error so extreme it approaches the theological. The reviewer is furious that death was not given better customer support.

---

**The nurses were nice enough I guess**

A concession structurally obligatory in the genre of the negative review: the reviewer must demonstrate fairness before proceeding to further grievance. "Nice enough" withholds praise even as it appears to grant it. Nice *enough* — enough for what? Enough to not be the target of this particular complaint. Enough to be mentioned in passing before being abandoned.

"I guess" casts doubt on the reviewer's own concession, suggesting that the nurses' niceness was either debatable or irrelevant to the harms being catalogued. Their niceness did not alter the outcome. Their niceness was not, finally, a form of medicine. The sentence dismisses its own content.

---

**but they kept saying things like "comfort measures only" which is basically code for "we're not even trying."**

The word "kept" introduces the reviewer's experience of medical language as repetition, a phrase the staff returned to so often it became a kind of chant. "They kept saying" — the staff's discourse was relentless, a wall of terminology the reviewer could not penetrate.

"Comfort measures only" is quoted in quotation marks, accurately representing a real clinical term used in palliative and hospice settings to indicate that curative treatment has been discontinued and all interventions are now directed toward the reduction of suffering. The reviewer translates this phrase as "code" — a deliberate obscuration, a way of saying something terrible in language designed to make it sound acceptable. "We're not even trying."

The word "basically" reappears. The reviewer employs it twice in five sentences, each time as a bridge between what was said and what was meant, between clinical language and the rage it provoked. The word serves as a hermeneutic tool: the reviewer is engaged in interpretation, in decoding, in revealing the hidden meaning behind the staff's obfuscations.

The translation the reviewer provides — "we're not even trying" — is not accurate to the medical meaning of "comfort measures only," which might be more accurately translated as "we are now trying to do something different and no less difficult than curing, which is comforting." But the reviewer has translated the phrase into the only vocabulary available to them: effort and its withdrawal, trying and giving up. In this vocabulary, the only opposite of giving up is trying, and trying means curing, and curing means living, and anything less than this is failure.

---

**The food was terrible and cold everytime**

"Everytime" is the review's first misspelling — a compound word pressed into a single unit, as though the frequency of the food's coldness had accelerated the reviewer past standard orthography. The word "everytime" does not exist in standard English, which separates the adjective from its temporal noun: *every time*. But the reviewer experienced the food's inadequacy as a single, continuous phenomenon — not as coldness recurring at each meal but as a condition of coldness that was always already present, that constituted the food's essential nature. "Everytime" captures this collapsed, undifferentiated experience with an economy the correctly spelled phrase would not achieve.

The food in a hospice is prepared for people who are dying, many of whom cannot eat, many of whom have lost the desire to eat, many of whom experience food's arrival as a reminder of the body's ongoing failure to sustain itself. The reviewer does not mention whether their mother ate the food or wanted it. The complaint belongs to a different register entirely — the hotel review, the restaurant appraisal, the consumer report. The reviewer has brought to a hospice the evaluative framework they would bring to a Holiday Inn, because it is the only framework they have.

---

**and she could barely swallow anyway so whats the point.**

The conjunction "and" links the food's quality to the mother's dysphagia — difficulty swallowing — as though these were two items in the same list of complaints, as though the cold food and the dying woman's inability to eat it were commensurate failures belonging to the same order of experience.

The word "anyway" is devastating. It functions as a concessive: the food was cold, and it wouldn't have mattered if it hadn't been cold, because the mother could barely swallow, and the reviewer knows this, and has included the complaint about the food anyway. The sentence contains its own refutation. The reviewer has written a sentence that knows it should not have been written and has been written anyway, because the act of complaint has become, by this point in the review, the only available form of grief.

"Whats the point" appears without apostrophe, without question mark. The missing question mark transforms the question into a statement, a flat declaration of meaninglessness that applies, in the reviewer's telling, not only to the cold food but to the entire enterprise: the hospice, the care, the nine days, the morphine, the dying. What is the point. The sentence does not ask. It announces.

---

**My brother drove 3 hrs from Reno**

The reviewer's brother, who has appeared nowhere in the review until now, emerges as a figure of pure pathos: a man who drove three hours — the numeral 3 accelerating his journey in the same way the numeral 9 accelerated his mother's dying — from a city associated in the American imagination with gambling, with luck, with the random distributions of fortune. He drove from Reno. From the city of chance to a building where chance no longer operated.

"Drove" is a verb of agency, of purposeful motion toward a destination. The brother did not merely arrive. He chose to go, covered distance, crossed landscape, to reach a mother who was dying in a facility where, the reviewer will explain, no one could direct him to a vending machine.

---

**and nobody even showed him where the vending machine was.**

The conjunction "and" again performs its terrible linking function: the brother drove from Reno, and nobody helped him find a vending machine. The sentence presents these facts as causally connected, or at least as belonging to the same ledger of harm. The brother's three-hour drive was met with institutional indifference to his need for a vending machine, and these two phenomena — the long drive and the unhelpful staff — are presented as equal partners in a single injury.

The vending machine is the review's most poignant object, because it dispenses comfort — food, drink, small satisfactions — in exchange for coins. It is a machine of the simplest possible transaction: you insert currency, you receive a modest pleasure. The brother, having driven three hours to witness his mother's dying, needed something from this institution, and what he needed was for someone to show him where the vending machine was. Perhaps he wanted a soda. Perhaps he wanted to not be in the room for a few minutes. Perhaps he needed an errand, however small, that could be completed successfully in a building where every errand involving his mother was failing.

---

**They gave her so much morphine she couldn't even talk to us which I think was on purpose.**

The review's central accusation. The reviewer believes the hospice staff deliberately administered enough morphine to render the mother unable to speak. The phrase "on purpose" indicates that the reviewer does not experience this as medical error or negligence but as intention. The staff wanted the mother silent. The staff dosed her into aphasia. The staff killed her voice before they killed her.

This is almost certainly not what happened. In hospice care, morphine is administered for pain and for air hunger — the subjective sensation of being unable to breathe, which is common in the final stages of dying. The dosages that produce comfort often also produce sedation. The mother could not talk because she was dying and because she was receiving medication to ease her dying. But the reviewer experienced it as silencing. They watched their mother become unable to speak and concluded that someone had done this deliberately, because the alternative — that no one is in control, that the morphine and the silence and the dying are just what happens — is unbearable.

"Which I think" is the review's most vulnerable locution — a hedge that acknowledges uncertainty even as the reviewer levels their gravest charge. "I think" is the language of someone who knows they cannot prove what they are saying, who suspects they may be wrong, who says it anyway because the alternative — that no one is in control, that the body simply shuts itself down without consultation or consent — is a fact they cannot yet hold.

---

**We asked multiple times for a second opinion**

A "second opinion" is a concept from curative medicine, where diagnostic uncertainty or treatment complexity justifies seeking additional expertise. The reviewer and their family asked for a second opinion about a dying woman. They asked multiple times. They asked because they did not accept the first opinion, which was that the mother was dying. They wanted another opinion, a better opinion, an opinion that would say something different.

The phrase "multiple times" introduces a counter, a tally of requests made and denied. The reviewer is keeping records. The reviewer is building a case. The reviewer has entered the hospice as a consumer enters any service encounter: with the expectation that persistence produces results, that the customer's complaint will eventually be escalated to someone with the authority to resolve it, that the answer *no* is a temporary condition that can be altered through repetition and force of will.

---

**and Dr. Patel just kept saying "her body is shutting down" like thats an answer.**

The coordinate conjunction "and" links the family's repeated requests to Dr. Patel's response, which the reviewer experienced as non-responsive. Dr. Patel — whose name the reviewer has remembered, who has become this review's antagonist — kept saying the same thing, just as the nurses kept saying "comfort measures only." The staff at Sunridge Hospice and Palliative Care spoke in refrains.

The phrase "her body is shutting down" is almost certainly an accurate clinical description of what was happening. The shutting-down of the body is the physiological process of dying, a cascade of organ failures that follows a well-documented sequence. Dr. Patel was describing reality. The reviewer heard a refusal.

The word "just" in "just kept saying" minimizes Dr. Patel's response, strips it of medical authority, reduces it to mere repetition — *just* saying, nothing more, nothing that would constitute help. And then: "like thats an answer." The demonstrative "thats" — missing its apostrophe — gestures at the phrase "her body is shutting down" as though it were an object that could be held up and examined. The reviewer looks at the phrase and finds it insufficient. Not an answer. A dismissal disguised in clinical vocabulary.

But "her body is shutting down" *is* an answer. It may be the only honest answer available in a hospice. The reviewer's sentence, unknowingly, records the exact moment at which the medical system's language failed to bridge the gap between clinical fact and human experience. Dr. Patel said what was true. The reviewer heard what was unbearable. There is no language that is both.

---

**The room was depressing**

A room in which someone was dying. The adjective "depressing" — flat, vernacular, devoid of specificity — is the most generic evaluative term available in the consumer's vocabulary. The same word is used to describe hotel rooms, restaurant lighting, weather, movies, and the condition of being alive. Here it is applied to a dying room. The room was depressing. The sentence does not know what to do with itself. It simply states the obvious, which is also, somehow, a complaint.

---

**and the chaplin never came even though we asked TWICE.**

The word "chaplin" is a misspelling of "chaplain" — the 'a' replaced with an 'i', the vowel narrowed, as though the word itself had contracted. The chaplain is the person in the hospice who attends to matters beyond the medical: prayer, ritual, spiritual comfort, the ceremonies of exit. The reviewer and their family asked for the chaplain, and the chaplain never came. This is the review's most credible grievance, its most legitimate accusation of institutional failure, and it is presented in a sentence that buries it among complaints about cold food and vending machines and the depressive quality of the room's atmosphere.

The word "TWICE" — capitalized, echoing the "NOT" of the review's opening — marks the second appearance of emphasis-through-capitalization. The capitalization performs the experience of having to ask more than once, of the request having been made loudly, of the family having used their outside voices in the halls of the institution and still having received nothing. Twice they asked. The chaplain never came. No prayer was said, or if it was said, it was said by the family themselves, in the depressing room, beside the mother who could not swallow, while the morphine did its work.

---

**There was a dog that came around one time which was fine but we didn't ask for that.**

The dog is the review's most structurally complex figure. The reviewer acknowledges the dog was "fine" — the only unambiguously non-negative assessment in the entire review — but immediately qualifies this assessment: "we didn't ask for that." The sentence performs a rejection of comfort that was not requested, of solace that arrived uninvited, in a form the family could not control.

Therapy dogs in hospice settings are trained to offer non-verbal comfort — presence, warmth, the pressure of a body against a body, the simple fact of a living creature that asks nothing and requires nothing beyond being touched. The dog came around one time. It was fine. But the family did not ask for it. The family asked for a second opinion, for a chaplain, for someone to show the brother from Reno where the vending machine was. Instead they received a dog. The review records this substitution with precision and with a rage that has, by this point in the text, become almost indistinguishable from grief.

The sentence's structure — *which was fine but* — is the structure of a gift rejected not because it was unwelcome but because it was insufficient, because it was the wrong thing arriving at the wrong time, because a dog came into a room where a woman was dying and this was supposed to be enough.

---

**My mother deserved better then this place.**

The word "then" is a misspelling of "than" — a common error in American informal writing, in which the comparative "than" is replaced by the temporal "then," as though the comparison were happening in time. The reviewer's mother deserved better *then* — at that time, in that moment, during those nine days — rather than better *than*, which is a spatial comparison, a placement of one thing above another. The misspelling is accidental but the result is a sentence in which the temporality of the dying — the *when* of it — overtakes the evaluative comparison — the *than* of it — as though the reviewer's most urgent meaning is not that the place was inferior but that it happened *then*, at a time that cannot be revisited or revised, a time that is now over.

"My mother deserved better" is the sentence's core, and it is a sentence with which no one could disagree. Every dying person deserves better. The sentence is true and also useless, because "better" is not a condition that can be provided to someone who is dying, because dying is the worst thing and there is no "better" version of it, because what the reviewer means is that their mother deserved not to die, and this is a service the hospice could not provide.

---

**My family will not be donateing.**

"Donateing" is the review's final misspelling — a present participle constructed with an unnecessary 'e', the gerund of a gift refused. The reviewer's family will not be donating to Sunridge Hospice and Palliative Care. This is the review's final, culminating act: a financial withholding, a refusal to support the institution that failed to save their mother. The sentence is written in the future tense, as though the reviewer is already composing the letter they will send, already imagining the check they will not write, already practicing the refusal that is the only power they have left.

The sentence's grammar is slightly irregular — "will not be donateing" rather than "will not donate" — and the irregularity stretches the refusal across time, making it a continuous act rather than a single decision. The family will not be donating. They will continue to not donate. Their refusal is ongoing, perpetual, a no without end.

---

**Would not recommend.**

The review's final sentence repeats its opening, minus three exclamation points and one capitalization: "Would NOT recommend!!!" becomes "Would not recommend." The loss of "NOT" to the decapitalized "not," the loss of all three exclamation points, transforms the verdict from a cry into a murmur, from accusation into weary restatement. The ring composition closes. The reviewer has said everything they came to say — about the food and the room and Dr. Patel and the morphine and the dog and the chaplain who never came — and arrived back at the beginning, which is also the end, which is the only sentence in the review not about the reviewer's mother but about the institution. The mother has, by this point in the text, been almost entirely displaced by the grievances. The review has become an elegy for an experience rather than for a person. The reviewer is writing not about their mother's death but about their own failure to alter it, to improve it, to render it acceptable through complaint, to make of dying something other than what it is: a process during which no one is in charge and from which there is no second opinion and toward which no vending machine can direct you and over which the body presides in absolute and indifferent authority, shutting itself down, shutting itself down, shutting itself down.
