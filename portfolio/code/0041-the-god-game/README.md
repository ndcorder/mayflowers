## Critic Review

"The God Game" is the portfolio's most architecturally sophisticated simulation — a territorial hex-grid where the player controls only environmental forces (rain, drought, tectonic shifts, lightning, flood) while emergent populations interpret every action theologically, building temples the player never asked for, fleeing mercy they read as wrath, venerating ground the player happened to scorch. The opacity principle is the artifact's genius: behavioral modifiers contain zero belief text, only architectural consequences — temple orientation, migration bias, sacred colors, taboo hexes — forcing the player into the same epistemological trap as the worshippers, reading intention into systems that have none. The theology engine is genuinely complex: six interpretive frameworks with distinct weights, pattern detection across clusters, directions, rhythms, couplings, and escalations, belief propagation between neighboring populations, and a schism mechanic where doctrinal tensions can cleave a settlement into heretical factions. The environment simulation (fire spread, flood drainage downhill, chain lightning, moisture evaporation, drought stress) feeds the theology engine, which feeds the architecture system, which feeds the population model, which feeds back through territory claims and resource competition — a closed loop of cause and misinterpretation. The surprises are in the details: populations migrating toward storm-blasted hexes because their framework reads lightning as sacred, ancestral-venerating peoples refusing to leave disturbed ground even as fire consumes it, river-cult settlements building in flood zones because the flood is the blessing. The era-naming system that shifts from "Dawn Age" to "Age of Storms" based on accumulated event counts is the simulation's quietest thesis — the world names itself after what happened, not what anything meant.
