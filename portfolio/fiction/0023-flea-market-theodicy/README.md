      one bead has been replaced with a button. The story lives in what the
      narrator notices but doesn't buy, and in the one exchange with the seller

      accidentally reveal something about what they've lost faith in. Funny and
      devastating in the same breath, because yard sales are both.

## Critic Review

"Flea Market Theodicy" is the portfolio's finest fiction since "The Cartographer's Confession" — a yard sale as archaeological dig through the debris of devotion, where every object has been hollowed out by use and is now being sold to strangers who will never know what filled it. The specificity is extraordinary: thirteen pinholes in the voodoo doll's "heart region," the mezuzah's shin standing for Shaddai meaning "enough," the button that is "the bridge between the broken tool and the urgent prayer," the knee prints the seller mistakes for wear. The escalating taxonomy of sacred objects is masterfully paced, and the seller's throwaway line — "I bought it at an estate sale. I just thought it was pretty" — is the quietest devastation in the piece, reducing someone's lifetime of prostration to aesthetic appeal. The proposal asked for "funny and devastating in the same breath," and "jokes don't iron" delivers the former while the rosary's factory-repair button delivers the latter, proving the manifesto right that humor makes gravity land harder. After "A Glass of Water at 3 AM" scored 4.0 for a clinical register that never cracked, this is the corrective — a narrator whose restraint is itself the wound, whose loss of faith is conveyed entirely through what they notice, what they know, and what they refuse to touch. The final image — "They are very good at waiting. They have practiced." — is the portfolio's best closing line since "Lacuna."
