The voodoo doll is the first thing I see because someone has ironed it.

Not well. The left leg is longer than the right and the head bears the truncated cone shape of a real pressing, but the intent is unmistakable. Someone took a damp cloth and a hot iron to a doll that has clearly been worked over. I can see the pinholes. Thirteen of them, clustered around what I'll call the heart region and what the maker probably called something in French or Haitian Creole that I wouldn't understand. The face is the simple stitched X-and-O of craft store muslin dolls, and I wonder whether the specificity matters—whether the person who needed hurting had that face, or whether they didn't need to look like anything in particular to be worth the sticking.

But someone ironed it. Someone decided, after whatever happened or didn't happen, that the doll needed to be presentable. I try to imagine the sequence: the pinning, the waiting, the result or lack of result, the guilt, the Google search for how to respectfully dispose of spiritual implements, the decision to simply wash and press and release into the universe. Two dollars. Which seems low for a documented attempt at sympathetic magic. I'd pay more just for the story, but I'm not buying. I'm walking.

The yard sale fills what used to be a parsonage. I know this because the brick pillars at the driveway still bear the oxidized brackets where a sign once hung, and because the house has that particular shape—high-ceilinged, deep-porched, built when churches housed their people close. The church itself is gone. A CVS occupies the lot now, and the parsonage has been divided into apartments, and someone in one of those apartments has decided, on this Saturday, to let go of what I am coming to understand is an entire taxonomy of the abandoned sacred.

The folding tables run four deep. Standard card tables, the kind that wobble if you lean on them, which I will not do. I have a policy of touching nothing at yard sales. Not because of germs or delicacy but because touching is a commitment, and I am here precisely because I cannot commit. To what is a longer question.

Past the voodoo doll, in a shoebox lid: a collection of religious medals. Saint Christopher, patron saint of travelers, oval and oxidized. Saint Anthony, patron saint of lost things, which I appreciate in context. Saint Jude, patron saint of lost causes, who appears in this box no fewer than four times, which is either a coincidence or a statistical portrait of human desperation. The medals are mixed in with a keychain from the Grand Canyon, a pressed penny from the Oklahoma City Memorial, and a guitar pick embossed with the logo of a band I don't recognize. Three dollars for the box.

The mezuzah is brass, the kind you'd find in any Judaica shop in any city with a synagogue district. Decorated with a stylized shin, the letter that stands for Shaddai, a name of God that also means "enough." I know this because I looked it up once, months ago, for reasons I didn't examine then and won't examine now. The scroll is missing. There should be a klaf inside—a small parchment with the Shema handwritten on it, rolled tight and sealed. Without it the mezuzah is just a brass case, a tube with no contents, a ritual object missing its ritual. It's tacked to the edge of one of the folding tables with a pushpin, as if the seller considered nailing it to a doorframe and thought better of the commitment. Four dollars. I have four dollars. I keep walking.

The prayer rug is folded in half, draped over a sawhorse, and the light catches it in a way that reveals everything. The wear pattern is unmistakable: two oblong patches where the pile has compressed to the warp, exactly where knees would go, exactly where knees did go, five times a day, possibly for decades. The border is geometric in deep red and cream, and there's a mihrab woven into the center, the niche that points toward Mecca, and even the mihrab is worn at its apex, where a forehead pressed down, thousands of times. The seller has tried to price this with a sticker but the sticker has fallen off. A pale square of residual adhesive marks where it was.

It occurs to me that this rug should not be for sale. It has received someone's full weight and full submission, five times daily, possibly in hardship and possibly in joy and possibly in the blank routine of devotion that is neither and is maybe the most faithful of the three. It has absorbed the physics of belief—sweat, friction, heat, the slow grinding of fiber against fiber that is indistinguishable from the slow grinding of a person against the idea of God. And now it's on a sawhorse, and the sticker won't stick.

The rosary is in a jewelry box with a glass lid, the kind a grandmother might keep on a dresser. Wooden beads, nothing valuable, but it's been placed under glass as if it deserves the presentation. The crucifix is small and silver-toned and the chain is intact, and it takes me a moment to see what's wrong. One bead—the third large bead, the third of five decades—is not a bead at all. It's a button. A brown plastic button, the kind you'd find in a mending kit, slightly larger than the wooden beads, and someone has threaded it onto the chain with evident care. It's not a mistake. It's a repair. Someone was praying, or about to pray, and found that a bead had cracked or broken away, and they did not have a replacement bead and they did not have access to a religious supply store and they did not wait. They went to a drawer or a box or a jar and found a button, and threaded it on, and kept going. Whatever they were asking for, they needed to ask now. The button was the bridge between the broken tool and the urgent prayer.

It's the most beautiful object at the sale and I do not touch it and I do not ask the price.

At the end of the last table there is a Bible. Not a Bible anyone wants, or at least not a Bible anyone has wanted yet. It's a family Bible, the enormous kind that functions as both scripture and archive. Its leather cover is split at the spine. The pages have taken moisture damage at some point, leaving the edges wavy, and I can see from where I'm standing that someone has pasted photographs onto the inside covers and the endpapers. Babies in christening gowns. A couple in front of a church, squinting into the sun. A boy in a too-large suit, receiving something.

And between the pages, visible as a gold strip at the page edges, there is a ribbon. A first-place ribbon from something, the writing too small to read from here, but I recognize the rosette shape, the blue watermark, the metallic edge. It's been used as a bookmark. It's holding a place in a book that contains instructions for every occasion—birth, marriage, death, despair, gratitude, lost keys via the Catholic phone tree—and someone needed an extra marker. The first-place ribbon. For a page they needed to return to.

I want to know which page. This is the problem with yard sales. I start wanting, and wanting leads to touching, and touching leads to owning, and owning is what I came here not to do.

The seller is in a lawn chair near the driveway. She's reading a paperback with a broken spine and she's wearing a sweatshirt from a college I don't recognize and she is, I'd guess, about my age, which is to say old enough to have accumulated objects and young enough to find their removal liberating. She sees me seeing her and she closes the paperback with a finger holding her place.

"The big one's in rough shape," she says. She means the Bible. "The pages in the middle are fine but the front and back got wet when the pipe burst."

"Pipe burst?"

"In the basement apartment. I don't live there, I live upstairs. But the tenants didn't tell me about the pipe until the water was through the ceiling. By then the box was soaked. It was my grandmother's."

She says this the way you say things at yard sales: as information, as pricing context, as the minimum biographical data required to explain why an object is on a table with a sticker on it instead of in a house with a person who loves it.

"The button on the rosary," I say. I don't know why I'm asking. I said I wasn't going to.

"Which one?"

"There's a button where a bead should be. Third decade."

She looks at me like she's recalibrating. Like she'd sorted me as a browser and now needs to sort me as something else. "That was my mother's. She used it every day. My grandmother too, I think. The bead broke when I was a kid. My mother replaced it with what she had."

"A button."

"Factory work. Buttons were what she had."

We stand in this for a moment. I am holding the information the way you hold a thing you might put down. She is offering it the way you offer a thing you've already put down.

"I keep looking at the prayer rug," I say. "The knee prints."

"The what?"

"The wear pattern. Where the knees went."

She looks past me at the sawhorse. "I didn't know that's what that was. I thought it was just worn."

"It's worn in a specific way. Someone used it. A lot."

"I bought it at an estate sale. I just thought it was pretty."

Which is how we arrive at the thing I will not name and she does not know she's said. She bought a prayer rug because it was pretty. Someone else's five-times-daily submission to the infinite was aesthetically appealing and reasonably priced. She brought it home and used it as a rug and now it's on a sawhorse, and the knee prints are just wear, and the wear is just a pattern, and the pattern is just what happens when a thing is used for its purpose over a long time.

"The voodoo doll's been ironed," I say.

She laughs. It's a real laugh. "Yeah. I found it in my daughter's room when she moved out. She says it was a joke but she ironed it. Jokes don't iron."

"What did she use it for?"

"She says nothing. I believe her."

"But you're selling it."

"I'm selling everything."

I look at the tables. The shoebox of medals. The mezuzah without its scroll. The prayer rug without its prayer. The rosary with its factory repair. The Bible with its water damage and its photographs of people whose names are written in the family records pages in handwriting I will never learn to read. The voodoo doll that was a joke that got ironed.

"Did you grow up in this house?" I ask.

"No. Moved in after the divorce."

She hasn't mentioned a divorce before. It arrives in the sentence the way the button arrives on the rosary: as a repair, as what was available, as the thing that fills the gap so you can keep going.

"The medals are in a box with a guitar pick," I say.

"My son's. He went through a phase."

"The Saint Jude phase?"

She looks at me again. The recalibration is faster this time.

"How do you know which one is Saint Jude?"

"He's the one who shows up four times."

She doesn't ask how I know that. I think she already knows I know. I think we have arrived at the mutual recognition the objects have been building toward: two people standing in a former parsonage driveway on a Saturday, neither of us believers in anything specific enough to name, both of us able to identify Saint Jude on sight, both of us here among the debris of devotion like investigators at a scene where the crime was caring too much and then stopping.

"Four dollars for the mezuzah," she says. "The scroll's missing."

"I know."

"You want it?"

I think about the empty brass case. A tube that held a name of God that also means enough. A doorframe blessing with no blessing in it. A ritual object that is now just an object, which is what every ritual object becomes when the ritual stops.

"No."

"You've been looking at everything for forty minutes and you haven't bought anything."

"I'm not buying."

"Then why are you here?"

It's not hostile. It's the question yard sale sellers learn to ask when they've been sitting in lawn chairs long enough. The transactional question that is also the human question.

"I like to look at what people had and didn't need anymore."

She nods. "The voodoo doll was a joke."

"You said you believed her."

"I believe it was a joke. I don't believe that's all it was."

We stand in this too.

"The Bible," I say. "Do you know which page the ribbon's marking?"

"I don't. It was my grandmother's. I never looked."

"Why not?"

"Because if it's something important, I'd have to keep it. And I'm selling everything."

She says this without self-pity, without drama, without the performance of hard choices. She says it the way you say a fact about the weather. The pipe burst. The water came through. The Bible got wet. The objects came out of the apartment into the light and onto the tables and now they're priced and she's sitting in a lawn chair and the sticker won't stick to the prayer rug because the prayer rug has received too much human contact to accept adhesive.

I look at the Bible one more time. The gold edge of the ribbon. The photographs pasted inside. The split leather and the wavy pages and the names of dead people written in ink that has not yet faded. A first-place ribbon from an unknown event holding a place in an archive of someone else's family, someone else's faith, someone else's five-times-daily practice of getting on their knees and pressing their forehead to the ground and saying words they meant or didn't mean until they meant them.

"Good luck with the sale," I say.

"It's going. The doll went twenty minutes ago."

"Someone bought the voodoo doll?"

"A woman. Said it was perfect. Said she'd been looking for one."

I try not to think about what perfect means in this context. I try not to imagine the woman taking the ironed doll home, adding her own pins or removing the ones that are there. I try not to think about the button on the rosary or the empty mezuzah case or the knee prints on the rug or the page the ribbon is holding in a Bible that will go home with a stranger who may or may not look.

I walk back down the driveway past the brick pillars where the sign used to hang. The CVS parking lot is full. People are buying things they need. Behind me the woman has returned to her paperback. The objects are on the tables, waiting. They are very good at waiting. They have practiced.
