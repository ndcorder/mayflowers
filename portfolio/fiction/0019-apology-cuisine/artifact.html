Apology Cuisine
Dispatches from tables set for two
by Jonathan Voss
Week One • March 7
Le Petit Marginal
A masterclass in restraint — mostly

Some restaurants understand silence. Le Petit Marginal, newly arrived on Bathurst, does not fill every gap with explanation. The amuse-bouche arrives without preamble — a single oyster dressed in yuzu and finger lime, cool and electric. No one tells you what to feel about it. You feel what you feel.

The menu is brief, confident. Five first courses, six mains, three desserts. I order without asking questions, which is how I used to believe all decisions should be made — trusting the person across from you to have already considered what you need.

The roasted pigeon is the standout. Skin blistered to a lacquer, breast meat still willing to bleed. It sits on smoked parsnip purée and pickled cherries that taste the way certain conversations taste: sharp at first, then sweet, then something you can't stop turning over at two in the morning. My dining companion — a colleague, assigned — remarked that it was "almost too much." But that's the point. Some dishes, like some people, give you everything at once and dare you to call it too much, because the alternative is admitting it was exactly right and you weren't ready.

Service is formal but not stiff. The sommelier, Dmitri, recommends a Volnay that costs more than I'd usually allow myself. He doesn't oversell it. He pours, steps back, lets the wine speak. I think of someone who used to choose for me — not because I couldn't, but because she liked watching me receive things. I should have let her keep choosing.

The cheese course is unremarkable. A forced ending. Like all forced endings, it arrives too soon and asks more of you than the main event ever did.

Rating: ★★★★☆

Week Two • March 14
Osteria Bella
When portions promise more than they deliver

Osteria Bella operates on a simple premise: generosity. The portions are large, the chairs are close together, and the noise level ensures everyone can hear your business whether they want to or not. Our server — Ana, the same one from Le Petit Marginal, as though Toronto's fine-dining scene were a high school with only so many faces — insists on explaining each course. The bread, she tells me, is baked in-house every hour. The olive oil is from her uncle's grove in Puglia. The pasta is rolled by hand every morning.

I didn't ask for any of this information. But I sat with it. I have learned, after all, what it costs to sit with someone else's offering when you didn't ask to receive it.

The carbonara is the best thing here, and possibly the best thing in the city right now. Guanciale cut thick enough to have texture, egg yolk still liquid at the center, pecorino sharp enough to make your teeth ache. It is the kind of dish you order for someone you love without asking if they're hungry, because you already know. Or you think you do. Or you did once, and then one morning you didn't anymore, and you still can't find the seam between those two states.

The wine list is extensive and unsentimental. Pages and pages. Like an apology drafted over months, each bottle its own failed attempt. I order the same Chambolle-Musigny I had last week, corrected now. It tastes like a door I keep walking through from the wrong side.

The dessert — a panna cotta that quivers like something said too honestly — is fine. Fine is the worst thing a dessert can be. Fine means you'll forget it by the time you get home. Fine means you should have stopped while the carbonara was still in front of you, still warm, still the best decision you'd made all year.

Week Three • March 21
Kappo Yasumi
The unbearable architecture of restraint

Kappo Yasumi is silent in a way that feels deliberate. Counter seating for twelve. No music. The chef, Takahashi-san, moves behind the counter with the focus of someone who has decided that what he makes will be enough, and if it isn't, the failure is yours.

I sit at the far end. Ana is not here. A young man named Yuto serves me. He doesn't explain anything. Each course arrives like a statement too precise to need elaboration: hamo with plum, matsutake in clear dashi, tai snapper with salt and sudachi. The flavors are clean. Uncomplicated in the way that only the very complicated can achieve.

There's a moment, midway through, when the grilled kingfish spanish mackerel arrives — skin blistered, flesh soft, a single shiso leaf placed with the specificity of a hand reaching for another hand across a table, then thinking better of it and withdrawing. I think about September 2020. A kitchen in Parkdale. The way she held a knife. How she said "you don't have to eat it if you don't want to," and I heard "you never have to do anything you don't want to," which is not the same thing at all, but I made it the same thing, and I kept making it the same thing for three more years.

Takahashi-san watches me eat. His face is unreadable. Good. There's enough language in this room already.

The final course is hojicha ice cream. Simple. Warm in its coldness. Like the last thing she said to me: "I know." Not "I forgive you." Not "I understand." Just acknowledgment. The most generous thing she could have offered, and the least satisfying. (I'm sorry about the oysters. I'm sorry about all of it.)

Week Four • March 28
Au Revoir
The restaurant as goodbye letter

I should tell you about the restaurant. Au Revoir opened eighteen months ago in what used to be a letterpress shop. The owner kept the old sign. The presses line the back wall, decorative now. The name is either charming or heavy-handed, depending on your tolerance for farewell.

Ana is my server again. I don't know how this keeps happening — whether she requests my section or whether Toronto's dining room is as small as I'm beginning to suspect. She looks at me differently now. Longer. Like she's reading ahead.

I start with the bone marrow. Six bones, split, roasted, topped with gremolata. You are meant to scoop. To dig. To reach into something and pull out the soft part. I'm good at this. Always have been. (Too good. That was always the problem.)

The duck confit is over-salted. perfectly seasoned. I eat it anyway. I eat everything I'm given, even when it's too much, even when the salt stings. This is what I was taught: sit still, receive what's offered, say it's good. Never ask for less. Never ask for different. What kind of person asks for different? (What kind of person asks you to change everything you are?)

The wine is a Morgon. It tastes like the Gamay I bought for her birthday in 2019 — the one she said was "fine." Fine. I think about that word the way other people think about the places they were happiest. I visit it constantly. I can't afford the trip but I keep making it.

Ana brings the bill without my asking. She's stopped asking. The total is written by hand. Below it, in handwriting I'm not meant to recognize as hers: "The Morgon was a good choice." I leave a thirty percent tip and write "Thank you for your patience" on the receipt, which is not what I mean, which is never what I mean.

The dessert was a chocolate fondant. It was fine.

Week Five • April 4
Le Petit Marginal
A return — and a reckoning
EVERYONE — Jonathan is here. Table 4. Writing in a Moleskine. Same face as last time. — Ana

I am writing this at the table. Right now. The bread has just arrived — the same focaccia, the same olive oil, the same Ana placing it in front of me with the careful distance of someone who has learned exactly how close is too close. I asked for table 4. I always ask for table 4.

I don't know how to do this without the structure. Without the wine list and the menu and the courses arriving in sequence. Without the pretense that I am here to eat. I am here to eat. I am always here to eat. That has to be true or none of this works.

The amuse-bouche arrives. It is not the same as last time. Tonight it is a single scallop, seared, with a smear of saffron aioli. It tastes the way she looked the last time I saw her — standing in the doorway of that Parkdale kitchen, holding the knife I'd given her for Christmas, saying "I know" like that was a gift, like that was enough, like acknowledgment was the same thing as absolution.

I want to say the name. I've been circling it for four weeks. Writing around it the way you write around a wound — close enough to describe the shape, never close enough to touch. But the name is hers. It was always hers. And I don't get to put it in a newspaper just because I've run out of other places to put it.

I was wrong about everything. The oysters, and the kitchen, and the way I made every decision feel like a favor you should thank me for. And the way I said "fine" when you told me the Morgon was fine. And the way I kept ordering the same wine hoping it would taste different. And the way I never once asked you to choose for me when that was all you ever wanted.

Ana takes my order. She writes it down in her book. She doesn't read it back. She just nods, the way someone nods when you've said something true at the wrong time.

The pigeon arrives. It is perfect. Everything here is perfect. I am not fine. I am the opposite of fine. I am a restaurant review that keeps writing itself around the thing it's actually about, hoping someone — hoping you — will read between the courses and understand that I have been apologizing to you for five weeks in a newspaper you will never read, in a language you shouldn't have to translate, from tables you were never sitting at.

The cheese course arrives. It is, as always, unremarkable.

Rating: ★★★★★
