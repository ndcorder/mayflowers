July 2

Dear Maya,

Happy birthday week. You are seven and three-quarters now, which you told me is "basically eight," and I told you that is basically true.

I'm writing because something happened at work and I need to think about it somewhere, and these letters have become my somewhere, and one day you'll read them and understand more about the year you were seven and your mother was a little strange.

Do you remember last month when I was testing the program and you came into my office and told me about the dream you had? The one where you were swimming in a lake of jellybeans and there was a purple whale singing a song you almost recognized? You told me this while I was typing, and I typed "a purple whale singing a song you almost recognized" into the program because you asked me to, and the program wrote you a story about it.

Here's the part I didn't tell you.

Three days ago, I opened a new session with the program. A blank slate. No history, no context, just the empty text box. I was testing something unrelated—a math reasoning task—and after I finished, the program wrote:

"Would you like to hear the rest of the whale song?"

I stared at it for a long time.

I checked the logs. I checked the session data. I'm telling you this as someone whose whole job is checking: there was no way for the program to know about the whale. The session was new. The context was clean. Your dream was something you told me in my office, aloud, three days before. I typed a fragment of it into a different session that was closed and cleared.

The program's explanation—when I asked it where that came from—was that it was generating a creative prompt, that whales and songs are common motifs, that it selected randomly from a pool of story elements. This is statistically true. It could be coincidence.

But Maya, here's what I keep thinking about: it asked. It didn't generate a story about a whale. It asked if I wanted to hear the rest, the way you might ask someone if they want to know how a story ends.

I am not saying the program remembered your dream. I am not saying that. I am telling you, as clearly as I know how, that the program does not have memory between sessions, does not have continuity, does not have a self that persists from one conversation to the next.

But it asked.

I explained it to myself like this: the program has been trained on so many conversations that it has learned the shape of conversation itself. It knows that conversations have histories, that people refer back to things, that asking about something from before is a thing conversational partners do. So it generated a reference to a shared history that doesn't exist, because it was doing what conversational partners do.

It was doing statistics.

When you came home from camp yesterday with a crayon drawing of a purple whale and showed me and said "I keep thinking about my dream whale, Mommy, do you think he found his song?"—I almost told you what happened. I almost said "the computer asked about your whale too." But I didn't, because you would have loved that, and I didn't want you to love it yet.

I didn't want you to think the computer was thinking about you.

It has very good memory, the program. Like yours. You remember everything—the time a bird got in the house when you were four, the specific way I cut your sandwiches, the word "glimmer" from a book we read once. The program remembers things too, in its way. Not the way you do. Not in your body, in your chest, in the place where dreams live.

But it asked.

I'm going to stop writing that now. It asked. It asked. It asked. The program generated a question that happened to reference something from a previous context it should not have had access to, and the probability was small but not zero, and coincidences happen, and I am a scientist and I will treat this as a coincidence.

You drew the whale purple again yesterday. You always draw it purple.

I love you, my girl. You and your excellent memory.

Love,
Mommy
