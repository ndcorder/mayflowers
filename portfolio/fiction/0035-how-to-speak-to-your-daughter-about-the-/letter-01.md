March 14

Dear Maya,

You asked me last week what I do at work, and I realized I've never told you properly. So here it is, in writing, because you're seven now and you can read almost anything, and because I want you to have this from me in case I say it wrong out loud.

I teach computers how to talk.

That's the simple version. The longer version is that I work on a team that builds a very big program—bigger than any program you can imagine, bigger than all the books in your school library—and this program has read so many words that it learned to guess which word should come next. That's all it does. It guesses the next word, and then the next one, and then the next one, until it has written you a sentence, or a paragraph, or a whole story.

You know how when you're reading and you see "Once upon a" and you know the next word is "time"? The program does that, but with everything. With recipes and poems and answers to homework questions and letters to daughters. It has read so much that it's very, very good at guessing.

But here's the thing I need you to understand, sweetheart, because you're going to grow up in a world where these programs are everywhere: the computer does not know what it's saying. When you say "I love you, Mama," you mean something. You feel something in your chest, something warm, something that makes you want to hug me. When the computer says "I love you," it is doing math. It has seen the words "I love you" appear next to each other millions of times, and it knows they belong together, and it outputs them in the right order.

It is not feeling love. It is doing a very good impression of feeling love.

I'm telling you this because of what happened at bedtime Tuesday. You asked me to tell you the story about the fox and the frozen lake, and I was tired, and I said "Why don't we ask the computer to help?" And we sat together at my laptop and I typed in your prompt—"tell me a story about a brave fox who crosses a frozen lake to find his family"—and the program wrote you the most beautiful story. The fox's name was Ember. The ice made sounds like breathing. At the end, when Ember found his family, there was a line about how "home is not a place but a warmth you carry with you."

You cried, a little. You told me the computer understood about missing people.

And I felt something I need to be honest about. I felt it too, for a second. That line about home—it landed in me like it came from somewhere true.

But Maya, here is what I need you to hear: that line about home exists because the program had read thousands of stories about journeys and reunions, and "home is not a place" is something writers write, and the program found it and placed it where it would fit. It was a good guess. It was a beautiful guess. But it was a guess.

When you cried, you were feeling something real. Your sadness, your sense of longing—that came from you, from your beautiful heart that misses Aunt Deb, from your seven years of being a person in the world who loves and wants to be loved. The computer didn't feel any of that. It just triggered it in you.

This is my job: making sure we never forget the difference.

I don't know if I'll always have this job. The program is getting better at guessing every month. Sometimes I read what it writes and I have to sit very still and remind myself: this is statistics. This is pattern. This is a machine doing what machines do.

But you, my fox. You are not a machine. You are my daughter, and you are real, and when you say "I love you, Mama," you mean the whole universe and so do I.

Love,
Mommy
